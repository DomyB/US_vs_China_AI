# Strata: executive summary

**China, the United States and Latin America's critical minerals, 2000 to the present.**
AI-assisted Data Analysis for Social Sciences, Sciences Po PSIA, 2026–27. Draft v0.1, 3 November 2026, written from data release 2026-10-29. The same summary, with every figure recomputed on each release, opens the site's home page.

How to read this document: every number below is computed from sourced records by the site's own code (UN Comtrade and CEPII BACI for trade, AidData for Chinese official finance, the DFC and EXIM for US finance, the IMF for investment positions, Congress.gov and the Federal Register for US policy documents, the team's statements dataset for what officials said). A number marked *model output* comes from a stated procedure (an index, a regression). The reading between the numbers is the team's interpretation, AI-drafted and not yet reviewed line by line by the team; it is labelled so on the site as well.

## Why the project matters

The minerals of the energy transition, lithium, copper, rare earths, nickel, graphite and niobium, come disproportionately from twenty Latin American countries, and the two largest economies compete for them. The usual account of that competition says three things: that the region is choosing sides, that Chinese loans buy its ore, and that US policy is winning it back.

Strata tests that account on the record rather than on the headlines. For twenty countries, twenty minerals and every year since 2000 it holds who bought each country's minerals, who lent and invested, how the countries voted at the United Nations, what their parliaments and leaders said, and what their press covered, with every number linked to its source and its reliability rating. It treats the region's governments as actors, not as the stage: they set the rules, choose partners and bargain over terms. And it keeps three layers apart on every page, facts, model outputs and interpretation, so a reader always knows which kind of claim is in front of them.

The result is a different picture from the usual account: the buyer of the ore is a structural fact that elections and loans did not move; the two powers hold different kinds of presence in the region; and the region itself mostly declines to talk about the rivalry at all.

## What we found

**1. The ore went to China, and it is a story of shares, not of value.** China's share of the region's reported mineral exports rose from 6% in 2000 to 44% in 2025, summed over the countries reporting both buyers (seven in 2000, nine in 2025); the US share fell from 16% to 13%. The value of those exports rose from US$17.4 bn to US$149 bn, with a peak of US$177.4 bn in 2021, but across the 26 years it moves with the copper price (correlation 0.95), so the political change is in who buys, not in how much the ore is worth. The regional figure is three national figures: Chile, Brazil and Peru carried 91% of the US$80.6 bn of reported exports to China in 2024, the other nine reporting countries 9% together.

**2. Beijing's money came and went before the trade peaked; Washington's attention came after.** Documented Chinese commitments to the twenty countries peaked at US$29.9 bn in 2015, when China took 32% of the region's ore; by 2021, the last year the source covers, they were US$3.9 bn and China's share was 48%. The money and the trade moved in opposite directions. US policy documents on the region's minerals peaked in 2021, at 62, against 27 in 2015; across the 22 years both series are known, their correlation with Chinese money is −0.04. Over 2015 to 2021, the record holds US$77.1 bn of Chinese commitments against US$7.1 bn from the DFC and EXIM, and 1.8 statements by US officials for every one by a Chinese official; 42 of the 46 US statements that take a position on China are negative. Words on one side, money on the other.

**3. The customer is Chinese; the shareholder is American.** In 2024 the United States held US$538.6 bn of inward direct-investment positions across the twelve reporting countries, mainland China US$5.1 bn and Hong Kong US$6.7 bn (all sectors; the IMF series does not split mining out). The usual objection, that Chinese ownership hides behind Hong Kong, does not survive the record: Hong Kong's position is the larger of the two in one of fourteen reporting countries (Brazil), and even added together the two Chinese figures are a rounding error beside the American one. Each power is strong where the other is absent.

**4. They are not buying the same minerals.** In 2025 China took the larger share in six of the nine core minerals, the ores its smelters and battery plants need: lithium 73% against 3% for the United States, rare earths 54% against 36%, copper 52% against 13%, niobium 49% against 8%, nickel 41% against 7%, and molybdenum. The United States led in three, the metals its own industry still refines: silver 61% against 15%, graphite 38% against under 1%, tin 17% against 14%. The rivalry over Latin American minerals is, in the trade record, two shopping lists that barely overlap, which means the region's leverage is mineral by mineral, not bloc by bloc.

**5. Politics did not move the ore.** After the 22 measurable changes of government in the six focus countries (four more lack enough reported years), China's share of the country's exports rose after five of the seven turns to the right and after eight of the ten turns to the left; net of the region's own movement the average change is +0.8 points after right turns and −0.3 after left turns, both within a point of zero. A panel regression of China's share on the previous year's Chinese finance, UN-vote alignment, electoral democracy, rule of law and mineral rents, with country and year fixed effects over 364 country-years, finds none of the five detectable (*model output*). Whatever the two capitals' levers do, it is small next to geology and prices.

## What is genuinely new or interesting

- **Elections are not a lever on the buyer.** The policy bet that a friendlier government in Buenos Aires or Brasília will bring minerals to the United States is a bet on something the record has never shown. A right-wing turn that wanted to redirect exports had nothing to redirect them with: the smelters are in China.
- **The debt-trap story and the resource-grab story are two different stories.** The lending collapsed while the purchases kept rising, so cutting the loans would not have cut the trade. Chinese purchases are a market position; the loans were a separate, shorter episode that ended before the trade peaked.
- **Washington's attention is counter-cyclical to the thing it describes.** The bills, strategies and orders arrived when Chinese lending had already fallen to a fraction of its peak, and the years after every family of US measures show the US share of the ore falling rather than rising. Policy has been aimed at a past position.
- **The region does not talk like a region choosing sides.** Of 200 statements by Latin American executives on their minerals, 72% name neither power (160 take no position on China, 143 none on the United States); leaders talk about royalties, lithium policy and value added. The friendliest words for Washington come from governments that sell it almost nothing (Argentina, Bolivia, Peru), while the largest seller to the United States, Mexico, is coded lukewarm.
- **The "lean to China" on the influence index is mostly a voting habit.** In 2024 the component that separates the two powers most is UN General Assembly voting agreement in 11 of the 19 indexed countries (*model output*); the export share decides it in five and the import share in three. UN votes correlate weakly with the mineral trade (0.22 across countries), and twelve countries that sell China little vote with it anyway, so a vote is weak evidence of a mineral tie.
- **The hidden route to the United States is real but bounded.** In 2023, 48% of US imports of the tracked minerals came straight from the twenty countries; at most 33% more could have passed through an intermediary, the largest ceiling being Canada at US$8.0 bn. The dependence is larger than US customs show and smaller than the strongest claims.
- **US pressure removes options and builds nothing.** In the companion study's sixteen documented cases of pressure on a Chinese-linked mineral project since 2017, the option was cancelled, frozen or restricted in seven, US pressure is named as the cause in one, and a US or allied alternative was built in full in none. For the host country the result is one option fewer.

## What to remember

Three countries' copper and iron, bought by Chinese smelters, owned largely by American capital, under governments that talk about neither.

## What the evidence can and cannot say

- Shares, turns and panels describe associations, not causes; prices and mine cycles move in the same years as the politics, and the site uses no causal language on them.
- The Chinese official-finance record ends in 2021 and the US record runs to 2026, so comparisons after 2021 are one-sided.
- The investment positions are all-sector; mining-only positions, or positions attributed to ultimate owners through the Caribbean centres, are what would overturn the capital finding.
- The statements are the team's selection, not a census, and their stance coding is unvalidated; the text model's stance scores are a zero-shot baseline awaiting the hand-coded validation sample. Neither enters the index.
- The forecasts to 2030 are indicative: in backtests the published models beat naive persistence by at most 13%, and their 80% bands covered 67% of outcomes at best.
