## How to read this

This text was drafted from the indicators on this page, not from outside knowledge. Every claim names in square brackets the finding or indicator it rests on; the numbers quoted are the ones the page shows and they change with each data release. It is an interpretation, bolder than the generated findings, and the project owner has not yet reviewed it. Treat it as a reading to argue with, not a result.

## What the contrasts show

The loudest voice is not the biggest spender [who_talks_who_pays]. US officials produce more than twice as many statements on South American minerals as Chinese officials, and more than nine in ten of their coded positions on China are hostile; Chinese officials speak less and only in praise of their own role. Over the seven years both finance records cover, Chinese institutions committed about twenty times more money to the twelve countries than the DFC did. This asymmetry is the central fact of the page: Washington argues, Beijing lends. The caveat matters: the Chinese figures include ports, power and budget support, and only about a sixth of the money is tagged to a mineral [talk_vs_money]. Even so, the mineral-tagged part alone exceeds the whole US record.

Warm words travel in one direction and the ore in the other [words_vs_trade_ARG, words_vs_trade_PER, words_vs_trade_BOL]. The executives of Argentina, Peru and Bolivia are almost uniformly positive about the United States in their coded statements of 2024–2026, while the United States takes a few per cent of their mineral exports and China a quarter to a half. Brazil and Chile are cooler in tone toward both powers and trade the most with China [words_vs_trade_BRA, words_vs_trade_CHL]. The reading: rhetoric toward Washington is a diplomatic register, not a trade signal. Where words and shares diverge most, the words are the cheaper of the two.

Leaders talk about the minerals they do not yet sell [talk_vs_trade_ARG, talk_vs_trade_BOL, talk_vs_trade_BRA, talk_vs_trade_CHL]. Lithium takes most of the mineral mentions in Argentina, Bolivia and Chile and rare earths in Brazil; the export value comes from gold, silver, copper and iron ore. The mismatch is largest where the promised mineral is least exported. This is not hypocrisy: politicians speak about what is contested and new. But it means the statements dataset measures aspiration while trade measures the past, and the two should never be read as the same thing.

Hedging is the regional posture, not the exception [hedging]. Argentina, Bolivia and Peru praise both powers and their executives record no coded negative toward China at all. Brazil stands apart with coded negatives toward the United States [words_vs_trade_BRA]. In a region where China buys and the United States talks, praising both costs nothing and keeps the option to sell to either.

The money did not move the minerals [money_without_trade, panel]. Nineteen large commitments, mostly Chinese and mostly outside mining, were followed by no shift in where the receiving country's minerals went. The panel regression says the same in general form: Chinese lending over the previous three years goes with a slightly lower, not higher, share of exports to China the next year. The simplest reading is that Chinese finance in this region bought infrastructure and goodwill while the ore followed prices and geology. Influence and dependence are kept in different ledgers.

Parliaments are mostly silent on the China question [parliament_vs_executive, cannot_see]. The legislatures' coded records take a position on China in a dozen or two documents a year, and where they do (Argentina and Brazil in 2026) they sound no cooler than the executives. The contest that US statements describe does not appear as a domestic legislative argument. Either the matter is settled between executives and companies, or the records do not capture it; both readings fit the data.

Attention without money [attention]. US policy documents on minerals in the warehouse tripled since 2008, and the DFC's commitments to the twelve countries peaked at about a billion dollars in 2022 before falling. The gap between the paper trail and the cheque is the US side of the first contrast.

## Future perspective to 2030

The published models expect persistence [fork_2030]. Under the baseline, China's share of mineral exports stays near half in Brazil, Chile, Peru and Ecuador and rises where the trend already points up (Peru, Ecuador). The scenario in which US sourcing rules bite, a shift of two points a year for five years, still leaves China with more than two fifths of those countries' exports; the United States reaches a quarter only in Chile. The arithmetic of parity makes the same point [parity]: for the United States to buy as much as China does in Chile, Brazil or Peru, about ten billion dollars of yearly exports would have to change hands in each, several times what it buys today. Copper in Chile and Peru and iron ore in Brazil could each cover the gap alone, which says where any real diversion would have to happen: in the two or three commodities that carry the region's trade, not in lithium or rare earths.

What history says about shocks is weak but not silent [shocks]: after the Chinese export-control measures of 2023–2024 and the US tariff and strategy measures before them, the share of exports going to China rose on average within two years. Those measures coincided with the pull of Chinese demand and did not reverse it. The event list is in draft and the windows overlap, so this is a direction, not an estimate.

Things to watch, each of which would show first in a layer this page tracks: whether the 2026 surge of statements, a third of the whole dataset, is followed by commitments in the DFC and finance records; whether the Section 232 copper tariff and the Chancay port move Chile's and Peru's shares in the 2026 trade data; whether Argentina's RIGI and Brazil's critical-minerals law bring the US-origin finance the statements promise; and whether the Chinese export controls of 2025 change the direction of niobium, graphite and rare-earth flows from Brazil.

## Bold readings, stated as such

- The United States is losing the mineral contest it talks about most, and the statements dataset records that talk, not a strategy with money behind it [who_talks_who_pays, attention].
- Chinese finance did not buy the minerals; prices and geology did. Dependence on China is a market outcome that the finance only accompanied [panel, money_without_trade].
- Hedging is rational and durable: no South American executive gains by choosing, and the data show none choosing [hedging, words_vs_trade_PER].
- Lithium and rare earths are political minerals; copper, iron ore, gold and silver are economic ones. Policy aimed at the first will not move the second [talk_vs_trade_CHL, talk_vs_trade_BRA, parity].
- The more democratic countries buy more of their mineral imports from the United States, but the data show no such association for what they sell. The US advantage in this region runs through what it sells, not what it buys [panel].

## What would change this reading

A validated text model that moved the legislative stances; a second coder on the statements that shifted the executives' scores; DFC or EXIM commitments after 2024 that approach the Chinese figures; a 2026 trade year in which Chile's or Peru's share to the United States rose by more than the scenario's two points; AidData's next release covering 2022–2024. Each would appear on this page before it changed the words here.
