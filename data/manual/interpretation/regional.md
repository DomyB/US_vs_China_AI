## Alignment

In 2024 the index leans most toward China in Suriname (+38), Peru (+34) and Bolivia (+31) and least in Paraguay (0) and Argentina (−13); ten of the twelve countries have an index that year, Uruguay and Venezuela do not [index_value:influence:2024]. The lean is made of two different things. In Peru, Chile, Brazil and Ecuador it is trade: around half of each country's mineral exports went to China in the latest year, against 3% to 14% to the United States [concentration:share_cn_x:2025]. In Suriname, Bolivia and Venezuela it is money: Chinese commitments that the mineral trade does not show [flow_finance:CN:2015-2021]. Documented Chinese commitments to the twelve reached US$195 bn over 2000–2021 against US$6 bn from US institutions over 1986–2024, in every sector [finance_event:CN] [finance_event:US]. The statements run the other way: US officials produce 2.2 statements for every Chinese one and nine in ten of their coded positions on China are negative [statement:count:bloc=United States] [statement:count:bloc=China]. My reading: Washington argues and Beijing lends and buys; the executives of the region praise both and sell to the buyer.

## Trajectory

Between 2019 and 2024 the Chinese index rose most in Brazil (+10.2) and Bolivia (+9.6) and fell most in Suriname (−13.2) and Argentina (−7.3) [index_value:influence:CN:2024]. Across the reporting countries in 2025, lithium (73%), iron ore (68%) and manganese (62%) are the minerals that go most to China; tin (17%), bauxite (14%) and copper (13%) go most to the United States [concentration:share_cn_x:lithium:2025] [concentration:share_us_x:copper:2025]. The panel regressions find no finance-to-trade link: Chinese commitments over the previous three years go with a slightly lower share of exports to China the next year (−1.2 points per standard deviation, wild bootstrap p 0.04), while a country one standard deviation more democratic buys 15.7 points more of its mineral imports from the United States [regression_result:exports_to_cn:twfe:finance_flow_lag1] [regression_result:imports_from_us:twfe:electoral_democracy]. The event windows, all around draft events, show the share to China rising after the Chinese export controls of 2023–2024 and after the US measures of 2017–2022, which in my reading means those measures coincided with the pull of Chinese demand and did not reverse it [event_effect:family=CN:export_control:CN] [event_effect:family=US:strategy:CN].

## Risks and likely next moves

The published models expect persistence: under the baseline China keeps about half of the exports of Peru, Chile, Brazil and Ecuador in 2030, and under the US-reshoring scenario still more than two fifths; the United States reaches a quarter only in Chile [forecast:export_share:CN:us_reshoring:2030] [forecast:export_share:US:us_reshoring:2030:CHL]. Parity in Chile, Brazil or Peru would take about US$10 bn of yearly exports switching in each, and only copper and iron ore could carry it [insights:parity_gap]. The next moves already on the record are the US–Argentina agreements of October and November 2025, Brazil's critical-minerals law of September 2026, the Section 232 copper tariff and China's suspended October 2025 controls [event:us_arg_swap_line_2025] [event:bra_lei_15506_critical_minerals] [event:cn_export_controls_oct_2025_suspended]. In my reading, the test of 2026 is whether the surge of statements (a third of the dataset is from 2026) is followed by commitments in the US finance record; if it is not, the contest stays one of words against ore.
